Ice Dams on Your Ontario Roof: What to Do Before the Melt Causes Damage

After a few freeze and thaw cycles, a ridge of ice can form along the edge of your roof. It looks harmless, but it can push water under your shingles and into your home. Here is what causes ice dams, what to check now, and what your home policy typically covers.

What is an ice dam?

An ice dam is a ridge of ice that builds up along the edge of a roof. It forms when heat from inside your home warms the roof, melts the snow sitting on it, and the meltwater runs down to the colder edge and freezes again. Over a few freeze and thaw cycles, that ridge grows. It looks like a harmless strip of ice, but it works like a small dam, and the water pooling behind it has nowhere to go but up and under your shingles.

Why a small ridge of ice can cause a big problem

Once water gets under the shingles, it can soak the roof deck, drip into the attic, stain ceilings, and damage insulation and drywall. The first sign is often a brown stain on a ceiling or a damp patch near an outside wall. Left alone, the same damp can lead to mould and to wood that softens over time.

Ice dams are most common after a heavy snowfall followed by a few mild days, which is a normal Ontario winter pattern. Homes with a warm attic, poor ventilation, or blocked gutters tend to see them more often.

What to check before the next thaw

The best fix is to keep the roof cold, so snow melts evenly instead of from underneath. Start in the attic. If you can feel warm air leaking up from the rooms below, that warm air is part of the problem. Sealing gaps around light fixtures and pipes, and making sure the attic insulation is even, helps a lot. If you are not comfortable going into the attic, a home inspector or roofer can check it for you.

Outside, clear your gutters before the snow settles in for the season. Gutters full of leaves trap water, and that trapped water freezes and adds to the dam. A roof rake, a long handled tool made for pulling snow off a roof from the ground, can take the snow off the lower edge after a big snowfall. Work from the ground, and never climb onto an icy roof.

Do not chip at the ice with an axe or a hammer. That is the fastest way to damage shingles and gutters. If the dam is already large or water is coming in, call a roofer. Some roofers use steam to melt the ice safely, and they can also look for the cause so it does not come back next winter.

What does your home policy typically cover?

If meltwater gets in and damages your home, your home policy typically covers the resulting damage to the building and your belongings, up to your limits, after you pay your deductible. The deductible is the amount you agreed to pay yourself when you bought the policy. The ice dam itself, and the cost of removing it, is usually not covered. That part is considered upkeep, and upkeep is the homeowner's job.

Keep photos of any damage and any receipts for emergency repairs, and tell your insurer or broker as soon as you find a problem. Small leaks are worth a call to your broker first, so you can talk through whether a claim makes sense before you file one.

A quick checklist before winter sets in

  • Clear the gutters and downspouts before the snow arrives.
  • Check the attic for warm air leaks and uneven insulation.
  • Keep a roof rake handy for the lower edge of the roof after heavy snow.
  • Watch for stains on ceilings and damp patches near outside walls.
  • Know your deductible.

Ice dams are a normal part of an Ontario winter, and a little attention now can save a wet ceiling in January. If you are not sure what your home policy covers for water damage, message me for a no-pressure second look. We can walk through what you have, before the next thaw decides for you.
