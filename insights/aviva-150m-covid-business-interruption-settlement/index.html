A $150 Million Settlement Just Closed Canada's Biggest COVID Business Interruption Fight. What Ontario Business Owners Should Check Now

On October 16, 2026, an Ontario court approved a $150 million settlement ending Canada's largest COVID business interruption class action. The settlement does not decide whether pandemic shutdowns were covered, but it holds practical lessons for every Ontario business owner about notice, endorsements, and indemnity periods.

On October 16, 2026, an Ontario court approved a $150 million settlement ending Canada's largest COVID-era business interruption class action. The case, against Aviva Insurance Company of Canada, covered about 49,000 policyholders across Ontario, Quebec, and British Columbia, and had been in the courts since July 2020.

The headline number matters less than what sits behind it. This settlement does not decide whether pandemic shutdowns were covered. It shows how much can turn on two questions you can check today: did you report a loss promptly, and did your policy include the endorsement you were counting on?

What the case was about

The businesses argued Aviva should have paid business interruption claims from COVID-19 shutdowns. The settlement covers only policyholders whose coverage included restricted access coverage, which responds when mandatory closures shut a business down, or negative publicity coverage, which responds to income loss from an infectious disease outbreak within 25 kilometres of the premises.

That narrow scope matters. Of about 49,000 class members, only about 3,600 gave formal notice of claims, and just 2,162 of those actually held the relevant coverage. The action was approved to proceed as a class action in March 2023 and was heading for a six-week trial in January 2026 before mediation produced a deal.

Why most class members are paid for 30 days

Under the distribution plan, most class members are paid for exactly 30 days of lost income, even if their policies covered longer. A Quebec medical practitioner with a 60-day policy argued this left roughly half her claim on the table, but the court held that a class action is a collective enterprise, not 49,000 individual claims heard together, and some claimants will recover less than full entitlement so the group can do better overall.

For business owners, the point is the indemnity period on your own policy: how long business interruption coverage pays after a covered loss, once any waiting period passes. A 30-day period and a 90-day period produce very different recoveries from the same shutdown, and that difference is set in your wording long before anything goes wrong.

What the settlement does not decide

State this plainly: the settlement is not an admission of liability by Aviva, and no court ruled in this case on whether those wordings cover COVID-19 shutdowns. That question remains open, even as $150 million changes hands. After fees and levies, remaining funds go to eligible class members through a claims process run by BDO Canada.

Class counsel receives $36 million plus $4.68 million in HST, a total of $40.68 million, and the Class Proceedings Fund takes an estimated $10.2 million. Each of the four representative plaintiffs receives $15,000. Do not draw a simple conclusion either way: the settlement proves neither that pandemic losses are covered under similar wordings today, nor that they are excluded. Coverage turns on your exact wording, endorsements, and the facts of the loss.

Lesson one: report a loss promptly

Aviva argued that roughly 95 per cent of the class, those who never filed a formal claim, had no right to recover and were too late to start. That notice argument was a central risk in the case. For any claim, policies typically require prompt notice, and waiting can create a dispute unrelated to whether the loss itself was covered. If your business suffers an interruption, notify your broker or insurer as soon as you reasonably can, record when you reported it, and keep documents showing the income you lost.

Lesson two: know which endorsements you actually have

A commercial policy is a bundle, and its endorsements decide what it responds to. Restricted access, negative publicity, contingent business interruption, and utility interruption can change the answer completely, and they are not automatically on every policy. At renewal, ask your broker to explain in plain language which kinds of interruption each endorsement covers and which it does not, especially if your business depends on a key supplier, a single location, or foot traffic a closure order could cut off.

Lesson three: check the period, the limit, and the waiting period

Three numbers shape most recoveries: the indemnity period, the limit or maximum payable, and the waiting period before coverage begins to pay. They interact. A generous limit with a short indemnity period may run out while you are still recovering, while a long period with a waiting period of several days leaves the first days uncovered. Judge them for your business: how long you could be closed, how quickly you could reopen elsewhere, and what your fixed costs would be meanwhile.

A short checklist before your next renewal

Answer five questions before renewal. Does your policy include business interruption coverage, and under what triggers? Which endorsements are attached? What are your indemnity period, limit, and waiting period? What would a month of closure cost in rent, payroll, loan payments, and other fixed expenses? If a loss happened tomorrow, who would you call first? If you cannot answer one from your documents, raise it with your broker before renewal, bringing last year's revenue and monthly fixed costs so coverage can be matched to a clearly described risk.

The bottom line

Five years of litigation ended with a settlement, not a ruling. The $150 million closes a chapter for Aviva and the class members sharing the fund, but leaves the coverage question for another day and leaves every other owner with the same homework: know what your policy says, report losses promptly, and check the period, the limit, and the waiting period before you need them.
