Business Interruption Insurance in Ontario: What It Covers When Your Business Has to Close

When a fire or storm closes a business, rent and payroll keep coming while revenue stops. Here is what business interruption coverage typically pays, what triggers it, and how to check your limit and indemnity period.

When a fire, flood, or storm forces a business to close its doors, the property damage is only part of the loss. Rent, loan payments, payroll, and taxes keep coming while revenue stops. Business interruption insurance exists to bridge that gap, but it is one of the most misunderstood coverages in a commercial policy. This guide explains what it typically covers, what triggers it, and which details to check before renewal.

This is general information about how business interruption coverage usually works in Ontario, not a promise about any specific policy. Wordings differ, so your own policy is the final word, and your broker can walk through how it would respond to your business.

What business interruption insurance covers

Business interruption coverage typically replaces lost net income and covers continuing operating expenses while a business cannot operate normally after an insured loss. Continuing expenses usually include rent, loan payments, utilities, and key payroll. The goal is to put the business in roughly the financial position it would have been in if the loss had not happened, for a defined period.

Some policies also cover extra expenses, such as the cost of renting temporary premises, running generators, or advertising that the business has moved. These extra expense coverages help a business reopen faster, which usually reduces the total interruption loss as well.

What usually triggers the coverage

Business interruption coverage is typically triggered by direct physical damage to the insured premises from an insured peril, such as fire, windstorm, or water damage. The property policy responds to the damage itself, and the interruption coverage responds to the income lost while repairs are underway.

Some policies extend further. Contingent business interruption can respond when a key supplier or customer suffers damage that disrupts your business. Civil authority coverage can respond when an authority orders an evacuation or closes access to your area because of damage nearby. Both extensions usually have their own limits and time caps, which are often shorter than the main coverage period.

The waiting period and the indemnity period

Two time periods shape every business interruption claim. The waiting period, often 24 to 72 hours, is the deductible measured in time: income lost during it is usually not covered. The indemnity period is the maximum length of time the policy will pay, often 12 months, and it starts when the loss occurs, not when repairs finish.

Choosing the right indemnity period matters. A restaurant that can rebuild in six months needs less than a manufacturer waiting on custom equipment with a year long lead time. Underestimating the indemnity period is a common gap, because the clock keeps running while permits, contractors, and supply chains catch up.

What business interruption usually does not cover

Business interruption coverage usually does not cover losses with no connection to physical damage at the insured premises or a covered extension. A slow season, a lost contract, or a market downturn is typically not covered. Losses from perils excluded by the property policy, such as flood where flood is not insured, are usually excluded from interruption coverage too.

It also does not cover income above what the business would have earned. Insurers typically look at financial records from before the loss, adjusted for trends, to calculate the loss. Keeping clean monthly financial statements makes that calculation faster and less disputed.

How to check your coverage before renewal

Start with the limit. Business interruption limits are often set as a dollar amount or as a percentage of annual gross earnings, and they should reflect current revenue, not revenue from years ago. A business that has grown since the limit was set may be underinsured without realizing it.

Next, check the indemnity period against a realistic rebuild timeline for your premises, including permits and equipment lead times. Ask whether contingent business interruption and civil authority extensions are included, and what their limits and time caps are. Ask about the waiting period, and whether payroll for key staff is covered during a shutdown.

Finally, keep records that make a claim provable: monthly profit and loss statements, tax returns, payroll records, and supplier contracts. If a loss happens, those records are what turn a coverage promise into a payment. Reviewing the limit, the period, and the records once a year is a small habit that protects the business when it can least afford a surprise.
