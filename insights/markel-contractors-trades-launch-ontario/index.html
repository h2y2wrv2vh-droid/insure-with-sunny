A New Insurance Option for Ontario Contractors: What Markel's Contractors and Trades Launch Means for Your Business

On October 13, 2026, Markel Insurance announced a dedicated Contractors and Trades sector in Canada, built specifically for contractors, trades, and specialist construction-related businesses. Here is what the new offering includes, and what Ontario contractors should take from it at renewal time.

On October 13, 2026, Markel Insurance announced a dedicated Contractors and Trades sector in Canada, built specifically for contractors, trades, and specialist construction-related businesses. For Ontario contractors, news of a new market entering the space is worth more than a passing glance. It is a useful prompt to look at your own coverage and ask whether it still fits the work you are actually doing.

Contractors are not generic commercial risks. A plumbing contractor, a roofing crew, an excavation company, and a millwork shop face different hazards, use different equipment, and sign different contracts. When coverage is assembled from a standard commercial package without attention to those differences, gaps tend to appear where it matters most: tools and equipment, work in progress, pollution exposures, and client contract obligations.

What the New Offering Includes

Markel says the new sector brings together casualty, property, and selected specialist coverage within a coordinated underwriting proposition, with the goal of helping brokers place multi-line contractors and trades risks through a more streamlined process.

The product scope includes Commercial General Liability, Excess Liability, and property coverage, with options for contractors equipment, tools, and installation floaters. Depending on the individual risk and the exposures presented, brokers may also be able to access specialist protection for pollution and professional liability exposures through Contractors Pollution Liability and errors and omissions coverage. Additional features may include business interruption, equipment breakdown, and selected coverage enhancements.

The launch also includes a partnership with ARAG Legal Solutions Inc., giving policyholders access to legal and risk management resources such as a legal helpline, contract review support, human resources assistance resources, and a legal document centre. For many contractors, contract review support alone is meaningful, since the contracts they sign often contain insurance requirements that deserve a second look before signature.

Why a Dedicated Market Matters

When an insurer builds a dedicated proposition for a segment, it usually means underwriters who understand the trade, wording shaped around real exposures, and an appetite for risks that a general commercial book may treat as outside the box. That can translate into better fit, more consistent decisions, and fewer surprises at claim time. It does not automatically mean lower premiums or broader coverage for every contractor. Each risk is still underwritten on its own merits.

For Ontario contractors, the practical takeaway is not to switch carriers on the strength of an announcement. It is to use the moment to review your own program against what a trade-focused offering is designed to address, and to ask your broker whether your current placement still reflects your work.

A Renewal Checklist for Ontario Contractors

Start with liability. Confirm your Commercial General Liability limit and check whether the work you do now matches the operations described on your policy. If you have taken on new types of jobs or started working on larger sites, the description may be out of date. Ask whether an excess liability layer makes sense given the size of the contracts you are signing.

Next, look at your tools and equipment. Contractors equipment coverage typically protects machinery and tools you own, while a tools floater can cover smaller items that move between sites and vehicles. Check that the limits reflect what you actually own today, not what you owned when the policy was first written. Newer equipment or a second crew with its own tool set may not be fully reflected.

If you supply and install materials, ask about an installation floater. Materials in transit, stored on site, or being installed are often outside the scope of a standard property policy. An installation floater is designed to cover that in-between stage, from the time materials leave your shop until the work is accepted.

Pollution exposure deserves its own question. Many contractors assume pollution liability applies only to environmental firms. In practice, a fuel spill, a release during a renovation, or contaminants disturbed on a job site can create a pollution claim. Contractors Pollution Liability is designed for these exposures, and it is worth asking whether your work creates one.

Professional liability is another coverage contractors sometimes overlook. If your work includes design input, advice, or project management, an errors and omissions exposure may exist alongside your general liability. Your broker can help you decide whether that exposure is real for your business.

Finally, review business interruption and equipment breakdown. If a fire, a theft, or a breakdown stopped your operations for two weeks, what would the financial impact be? Business interruption coverage is designed to help with lost income and ongoing expenses after an insured loss, and equipment breakdown coverage addresses the sudden failure of machinery and systems. For contractors whose revenue depends on a few key machines, these coverages can be the difference between a disruption and a crisis.

Subcontractors, Contracts, and Certificates

Two more items belong on every contractor review. First, subcontractors. If you hire subcontractors, confirm they carry their own liability coverage, collect current certificates of insurance before they start work, and check that your own policy reflects how you use them. A gap in a subcontractors coverage can become your problem on a job site.

Second, read the insurance requirements in your client contracts before you sign. Clients, general contractors, and municipalities often require specific limits, additional insured status, and waiver of subrogation. Those requirements may exceed what your current policy provides. Finding that out before signing is far less expensive than finding it out after a claim.

The Bottom Line

A new dedicated market for contractors and trades is good news for Ontario contractors, because choice and specialization tend to produce better fitting coverage. The announcement itself, however, is not a coverage decision. The decision comes from reviewing your own program against that checklist.

If your renewal is coming up, or if your work has changed since your last review, bring your current policy, your equipment list, and a sample client contract to your broker, and walk through the checklist above together.
