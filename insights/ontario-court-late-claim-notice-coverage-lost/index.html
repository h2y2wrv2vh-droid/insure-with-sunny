Waited Too Long to Report a Claim? An Ontario Court Just Showed What That Can Cost

A Toronto condo corporation asked a court to order its insurer to defend it in a lawsuit. The court refused, not because the claim was clearly excluded, but because the corporation waited nearly three years and eight months to report it. Here is what that decision means for your policy.

An Ontario court decision published this month carries a blunt warning for anyone who holds an insurance policy. A Toronto condominium corporation asked the court to order its insurer to defend it in a lawsuit. The court refused, not because the claim was clearly excluded, but because the corporation waited nearly three years and eight months to tell its insurer the lawsuit existed. By then, the case was almost ready for trial, and the court held the delay alone was enough to defeat the claim for coverage.

What Happened in the Case

The dispute began when a neighbouring business sued Metropolitan Toronto Condominium Corporation No. 1255, alleging it had impeded construction of a commercial unit next door. It held a liability policy with Intact Insurance. Rather than notifying Intact, the board decided, on the advice of its property manager, that the claim fell outside the policy. It chose to defend the lawsuit itself and said nothing to the insurer.

Years passed. Pleadings closed, documents were exchanged, discoveries and mediation were completed, and the matter was set down for trial. Only after a new board was elected and a director questioned the original decision did the corporation notify Intact. By then, nearly three years and eight months had gone by since the amended claim was issued. The corporation then asked the court to declare that Intact had to defend it.

Intact resisted on two grounds: an intentional acts exclusion and the late notice. The court did not need to decide the exclusion question. The delay was enough.

Why the Delay Mattered More Than the Exclusion Argument

Insurance policies require the policyholder to give notice of a claim promptly. Early notice lets the insurer investigate while evidence is fresh, appoint counsel, join settlement talks, and pursue other parties who may share responsibility. When notice arrives years late, those options are often gone.

By the time Intact learned of the claim, mediation had already happened without it, expert evidence could no longer realistically be prepared, and limitation periods for claims against other potential defendants had expired. The court treated the lost chance to take part in mediation as especially significant, since early insurer involvement can settle a case before trial.

The court also addressed a point that matters to every policyholder. The corporation argued that it had defended the lawsuit judiciously on its own. The court found that a general statement of that kind was not enough. Once delay is established, the law presumes the insurer was prejudiced, and the policyholder must prove otherwise with real evidence.

What Relief From Forfeiture Means, and Why It Did Not Help Here

Courts can sometimes excuse a policyholder who misses a policy condition. The legal doctrine is called relief from forfeiture. It can apply where a policyholder has substantially complied with a condition but fallen short. It is granted sparingly, and the policyholder must prove three things: that its own conduct was reasonable, that the breach was not grave, and that losing coverage would be disproportionate to the harm caused by the breach.

The corporation failed on all three. The court held it was not reasonable for a policyholder to deliberately withhold notice based on its own reading of the policy, then expect to notify the insurer late without consequence if it later changed its mind. The corporation had access to counsel and a broker, and had every opportunity to give notice sooner. On proportionality, the court noted that the difficulty of measuring the insurer's prejudice does not help the policyholder; it is the inevitable consequence of very late notice.

The Practical Lesson for Homeowners and Business Owners

Most people will never face a multi-year lawsuit. The lesson still applies to everyday claims. It is tempting to sit on a claim while you decide whether it is covered, whether it is worth claiming, or whether to handle it yourself. This decision shows that the waiting itself can become the problem. The court asks not whether you had a good reason to wait, but whether the insurer lost something because you did.

The same logic applies to a homeowner who gets a letter alleging damage from a fallen tree, a business owner who learns a customer was hurt on the premises, or a landlord who gets a demand letter from a tenant. In each case, the coverage question and the notice question are separate. You can report a claim and still dispute coverage later. What you cannot easily do is skip the report, handle the matter yourself, and then call on the policy years later when the bill grows.

What To Do When a Claim or Potential Claim Arrives

First, report promptly. Call your broker or insurer as soon as you learn of a claim, a threat of one, or an incident that could turn into one. If you are unsure whether something counts as a claim, report it anyway and let the insurer decide. Second, keep records. Save the claim letter, emails, photos, and conversation notes. Third, do not admit liability or agree to pay anything before speaking with your insurer, unless immediate action is needed to prevent further harm. Fourth, forward everything. Send new documents on as they arrive.

Finally, do not decide coverage on your own. Policy wording is often broader or narrower than it first appears, and exclusions are interpreted by courts in ways that are hard to predict. Your broker can help you understand what a policy may respond to, and the insurer decides coverage. Reporting a claim is not the same as admitting it is covered. It is simply putting the insurer on notice so the options stay open.

The Bottom Line

The condominium corporation may have had an arguable coverage position. It never got to test it, because it waited too long to report. That is the warning in this decision. Notice is not paperwork. It preserves the coverage you pay for. When a claim arrives, report it early, keep records, and let the coverage question be resolved separately.
