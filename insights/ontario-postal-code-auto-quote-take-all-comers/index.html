Your Postal Code Should Not Decide Whether You Get an Auto Quote in Ontario

Ontario's broker regulator just fined a major brokerage $50,000 for using the first three characters of a customer's postal code to decide who would even receive an auto insurance quote. Here is what happened, why it broke the rules, and what it means the next time you shop for coverage.

On October 22, 2026, Ontario's broker regulator fined PC Financial Insurance Broker Inc. 50,000 dollars and formally reprimanded the company. The reason was not a single bad quote. It was a filtering practice that ran for years: the brokerage used the first three characters of a customer's postal code to decide which leads it would accept from comparison websites and its own quoting platform. Customers in certain areas never received a quote from the brokerage at all. For Ontario drivers, where auto insurance is mandatory, access to a quote matters just as much as the price that follows.

The first three characters of a postal code are called the Forward Sortation Area. It identifies a general geographic area, not a street address. PC Financial Insurance Broker Inc., the auto insurance arm of the President's Choice Financial brand and a Loblaw subsidiary, bought a limited number of leads from aggregator websites, the third party platforms where consumers shop for quotes. The brokerage picked the leads it believed were most likely to turn into sales. Its filters meant consumers in certain areas were screened out before they ever saw a price.

Sometimes the filters reflected insurer concerns about loss ratios or profitability in specific areas. Other times the brokerage applied them based on its own business considerations, including lead conversion rates and marketing efficiency. Either way, a driver could enter their details on a comparison site and simply never hear from this brokerage.

That is where Ontario's Take-All-Comers Rule comes in. The rule requires brokers to offer prospective customers the lowest available rate from any insurer they work with, and it bars brokers from restricting access unless those restrictions match underwriting rules approved by Ontario's Financial Services Regulatory Authority, known as FSRA. PC Broker's postal code filters were never part of any insurer's approved underwriting rules. The discipline panel found that the practice ran from at least 2014 until April 12, 2022.

The Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario, known as RIBO, raised the issue with brokerages in February 2020, sending guidance to principal brokers reminding them of their obligations under the Take-All-Comers Rule. PC Broker did not change its practice at that point. It took a second warning, additional guidance from FSRA in November 2021 clarifying how the rule applied, before the brokerage acted. It removed the filters from its internal quoting system by around February 28, 2022, but the filters kept running on at least one aggregator site until April 12, 2022. The panel said it was deeply concerned with the length of the misconduct, and stressed that compliance matters especially because automobile insurance is mandatory in Ontario.

One detail in the decision is worth noting. PC Broker did not directly employ licensed insurance brokers and did not sell insurance itself. It outsourced brokerage services to another firm and operated as an intermediary layer between aggregator websites and the actual insurance placement. The rule follows the lead, not just the policy: a brokerage cannot outsource compliance to a filter on a comparison site.

You might wonder why a fine matters when the brokerage is already closed. PC Broker ceased operations in March 2026 and surrendered its licence effective April 30, 2026. With the brokerage already closed, the panel said the fine was primarily about sending a message to the wider industry. The 50,000 dollar fine is half the 100,000 dollar maximum that RIBO can impose on a corporate licensee.

So what does this mean for you as an Ontario driver? First, it is worth understanding the difference between a high quote and no quote at all. Where you live can legitimately affect your premium, because insurers use territory as one rating factor under their approved underwriting rules. A quote that seems high is not, on its own, evidence of improper filtering. What the rule protects is your access to the quoting process itself: a broker should not screen you out by postal code before checking what is available to you.

Second, if you are shopping for auto insurance, it pays to ask questions. Ask a broker which insurers they checked for you, and whether the quote you received is the lowest available rate among the insurers they work with. A missing result from one brokerage alone does not prove filtering, since lead limits and insurer appetite also affect results. But if you are repeatedly unable to get a quote at all, or you are told you cannot be quoted because of where you live without any explanation tied to an insurer's underwriting rules, get a second opinion from another licensed broker. A different brokerage may represent different insurers.

Third, keep a short record when you shop. Note the date, the brokerage or website you used, and what response you received. If you believe you were refused a quote improperly, that record makes it easier to raise the issue with RIBO or FSRA.

The broader lesson is simple. Ontario drivers are required to carry auto insurance, so the system that sells it has to be open to them. Insurers can set approved underwriting rules, and brokers can explain why a risk does not fit a particular insurer. What they cannot do is quietly filter whole areas out of the quoting process for marketing convenience.

The bottom line: A RIBO discipline decision handed down on October 22, 2026 fined PC Financial Insurance Broker Inc. 50,000 dollars for using postal code filters to screen auto insurance leads from at least 2014 until April 12, 2022. Under Ontario's Take-All-Comers Rule, brokers must offer the lowest available rate from the insurers they work with, and restrict access only in line with FSRA approved underwriting rules. If you cannot get a quote, ask why, get a second opinion, and keep a note of what happened.
