Ontario's September Storms: C$563 Million in Insured Losses and What Homeowners Can Learn

CatIQ's initial estimate puts September's southern Ontario storm outbreak at about C$563 million in insured losses. Here is what the damage patterns reveal about your home insurance, from hail-covered roofs to the fine print on basement flood limits.

Early September brought a storm outbreak across southern Ontario that many homeowners will remember for a while. After days of hot, humid weather, a line of severe storms swept from Windsor to Ottawa, delivering large hail, damaging winds, torrential rain, and confirmed tornadoes and downbursts. Toronto was among the hardest-hit areas, with ping-pong-ball-sized hail and damaging wind gusts. Severe flooding was reported in Vaughan and other communities north of the city.

On October 8, catastrophe data provider CatIQ released its initial estimate: about C$563 million in insured losses from the early-September storms. The figure covers commercial and residential property claims, vehicle claims, and loss-adjustment expenses. For Ontario homeowners, that number is a good prompt to check your own coverage before the next system moves through.

The September outbreak looks different from the July 2024 Toronto flash-flooding event, when a month's worth of rain fell in a few hours, submerged stretches of highway, and eventually produced a final insured-loss estimate of about $890 million. This time, flooding was reported, but wind and hail were the bigger drivers of losses, and average personal-property and auto claims are running below the levels seen in that earlier event.

That pattern surprises many homeowners. When people picture storm damage, they usually picture rising water, but wind and hail claims are often the larger event: torn shingles, cracked siding, broken windows, toppled fences, and trees coming down on roofs and cars.

Know how your policy treats that kind of damage. Hail damage to a roof is typically covered under a standard home policy, subject to your deductible, but older roofs are sometimes settled on actual cash value rather than replacement cost, which means depreciation comes off the payment. Before winter, check for missing or curling shingles, loose flashing, and damaged siding. If you are unsure how your roof is covered, that is a short question for your broker, and far easier to ask now than after the next storm.

One detail worth noting: industry watchers expected a higher estimate, and one theory is that caps on basement flood coverage held the total down, alongside newer separated sewer systems north of Toronto that limited flooding.

That is a polite way of saying what many homeowners discover after a storm: water damage coverage often comes with smaller limits than people assume. Sewer backup coverage is usually an optional endorsement with its own separate limit, and it is not automatically included in every policy. If you do not carry it, a sewer backup loss is typically not covered at all. Overland flood coverage is a different item entirely and may not be available or included in your area.

Find the sewer backup section of your policy, and check whether the endorsement is there and what the limit is. A five-figure limit sounds generous until you price out a finished basement rebuild. Also know where your main water shutoff is and confirm your sump pump actually runs. These unglamorous checks decide whether a storm is an inconvenience or a renovation.

It is natural to wonder whether a storm like this will push your premium up. Home insurance rates are filed with FSRA, Ontario's insurance regulator, and market-wide loss experience influences pricing over time. No single storm sets your renewal on its own, but a heavy loss year is one of the factors that shape what insurers need to charge across the market.

What you can control is your own risk profile and your deductible. Mitigation steps that reduce your exposure to the next storm include a backwater valve on the sewer line, a sump pump with battery backup, impact-resistant roofing when it is time to replace shingles, and keeping dead or weak limbs away from the house. Some insurers recognize specific mitigation measures; ask your broker which ones matter to your insurer and what proof they need.

Deductibles are the other lever. A higher deductible usually lowers your premium, but it also raises what you pay out of pocket the next time hail punches through your shingles. After a storm year like this one, revisit the tradeoff with real numbers instead of the default you accepted years ago.

October is still prime storm season in Ontario, and it pays to spend a weekend getting the property ready. Clear the gutters and downspouts so autumn rain has a path off the roof. Secure outdoor furniture, the barbecue, and anything else the wind could pick up. Trim branches that hang over the roof or the driveway.

Then document: walk the exterior of your home with your phone camera, then the key rooms and the basement. Store the photos somewhere offsite, such as cloud storage. Timestamped photos of your roof, siding, and finishes before the next storm make any future claim smoother. Keep receipts for big-ticket items and recent upgrades like the roof, furnace, and electrical panel. If you have never made a home inventory, a simple spreadsheet with photos attached is enough.

Some readers are still working through claims from early September, and a few reminders can help. File promptly rather than waiting to see whether the damage gets worse. Document everything before cleanup with photos and video, and keep receipts for emergency repairs to prevent further damage, since most policies cover reasonable emergency work. Do not discard damaged materials until the adjuster has seen them. Review your deductible first, so the claim math is clear before you file: if the damage is close to your deductible, talk to your broker about whether a claim makes sense.

If a claim is denied or paid for less than you expected, read the adjuster's written explanation carefully and call your broker. Sometimes the answer is an exclusion you did not know about; sometimes it is a settlement basis, like actual cash value, that deserves a second look.
