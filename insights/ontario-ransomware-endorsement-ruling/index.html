Ontario Court's Ransomware Ruling and Your Cyber Policy

On September 9, 2026, the Ontario Court of Appeal ruled that a ransomware endorsement's higher retention applied to a cyber-incident claim, even though the business had claimed under the base policy's general coverage. For Ontario businesses with cyber insurance, the message is clear: the event decides which policy terms apply, not how you frame the claim.

When a cyberattack hits your business, the way you describe the claim on the insurance form is not what decides your payout. That is the practical lesson from a recent Ontario Court of Appeal decision that every Ontario business owner with cyber coverage should know about.

On September 9, 2026, the Ontario Court of Appeal released its decision in Panasonic Canada Inc. v. XL Specialty Insurance Company, 2026 ONCA 633. The court reversed a lower-court ruling and held that a ransomware endorsement with a higher self-insured retention applied to a cyber-incident claim, even though the policyholder had submitted its claim under the base policy's general coverage provisions instead.

What happened in the case

The case goes back to a February 2022 ransomware attack on Panasonic Canada. According to Gardiner Roberts LLP's coverage of the decision, attackers gained access to Panasonic's network after an employee downloaded a malware-infected file. The attackers exfiltrated confidential data, posted it online, and demanded a ransom. Panasonic did not negotiate or pay. Instead, the company incurred approximately $2 million USD in response costs, including cybersecurity specialists, lawyers, network repairs, and replacement equipment.

Panasonic held a cyber risk policy from XL Specialty Insurance Company. The base policy carried a $1.5 million USD self-insured retention across most coverage categories. But the policy also included Endorsement #023, a Ransomware Sublimit Endorsement, which amended the base policy for cyber-extortion reimbursement and ransomware events and imposed a $3 million USD retention for those categories.

Panasonic submitted its claim under the base policy's general coverage and argued the $1.5 million retention applied. XL disagreed, arguing the claim arose from a ransomware event and was therefore governed by the endorsement's $3 million retention. The Superior Court application judge sided with Panasonic. The Court of Appeal reversed that ruling.

What the court decided

Writing for a unanimous panel, Justice Gillese held that the endorsement could not be read in isolation. Clauses 1 through 4 of Endorsement #023, the court found, unambiguously replaced the base policy's cyber-extortion and ransomware provisions. The definitions in clause 5 had to be read together with those clauses, not on their own. The court also pointed to clause 15 of the endorsement, which provides that where the endorsement's provisions are inconsistent with the base policy, the endorsement controls.

The court found that the attackers' communication to Panasonic fell squarely within the endorsement's definition of a cyber-extortion threat, and that Panasonic's response costs were a ransomware event loss, defined broadly as any and all loss for, arising out of, in connection with, or in any way involving a cyber-extortion threat. On the standard of review, the court applied correctness, noting the policy was a standard form used throughout the United States and Canada and that this was the first judicial interpretation of the endorsement.

The result: because the agreed claim amount was less than the $3 million USD retention, Panasonic's claim was wholly self-insured and the application was dismissed. The court awarded XL costs of $21,000 for the appeal and $88,000 for the application below.

The practical takeaway for Ontario businesses

As Gardiner Roberts put it, the decision is a reminder that an insurer's obligation to pay, and the retention that applies, is determined by the nature of the underlying event, not by how the policyholder characterizes the claim. You cannot sidestep a ransomware endorsement simply by pointing your claim at the base policy's general coverage.

That matters because many cyber policies in the Ontario market include ransomware-specific endorsements, sublimits, and retentions that differ from the rest of the policy. If a ransomware event triggers your policy, the ransomware language will usually be the language that governs, including for response costs like forensics, legal counsel, and system restoration, even if no ransom is paid and no claim is made under the endorsement itself.

What to check in your own cyber policy

If your business carries cyber coverage, this is a good reason to pull the policy out before your next renewal and look at a few things:

  • Find the ransomware language. Look for endorsements dealing with cyber-extortion, ransomware events, or ransomware sublimits. They often sit apart from the main coverage sections, and they can change both what is covered and how much of the loss you carry.
  • Know your retentions by category. Your policy may have one retention for general cyber coverage and a different, often higher, retention for ransomware-related losses. Make sure you know both numbers and what triggers each one.
  • Understand what counts as a ransomware event. In this case the definition was broad, covering any loss in any way involving a cyber-extortion threat. Your wording may differ, so read it.
  • Ask about response costs. Forensic, legal, and restoration costs made up the bulk of Panasonic's claim, and they still fell under the ransomware retention. Confirm how your policy treats incident-response expenses.
  • Talk to your broker before something happens, not after. A review of your cyber wording now is far less painful than discovering a high retention during an active incident.

Prevention still starts with people

One detail from the case deserves emphasis: the attack began when an employee downloaded a malware-infected file. Insurance can help your business recover financially, but it does not stop the attack. Ongoing staff training on phishing and suspicious downloads, regular software updates, and a written incident-response plan remain the most practical defenses, especially for smaller businesses that may not have a dedicated IT security team.

And if your business does not yet carry cyber coverage, this case is worth a conversation with your broker about what a policy would actually do for you. The wrong assumption is that cyber coverage works like a single deductible and a single limit. As this ruling shows, the structure inside the policy can matter as much as the headline numbers.

This article is general information for Ontario businesses, not legal advice. Policy wording varies, so check your own policy and speak with your broker or legal counsel about your specific situation.
