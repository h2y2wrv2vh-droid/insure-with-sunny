Errors and Omissions Insurance in Ontario: What It Covers When Your Advice or Work Goes Wrong

If you give advice or do professional work for clients in Ontario, one mistake can turn into a claim, even when you did your best. Errors and omissions insurance, usually called E&O, is the policy that helps when a client says your work cost them money.

If you give advice or do professional work for clients in Ontario, one mistake can turn into a claim, even when you did your best. Errors and omissions insurance, usually called E&O, is the policy that helps when a client says your work cost them money.

What E&O actually covers

E&O covers claims that your professional work, advice, or service caused a client a financial loss. Think of a consultant whose plan missed a deadline, an IT provider whose setup error shut down a small office for a day, or a bookkeeper whose error led to a tax penalty. The client is not saying you broke something physical. They are saying your work cost them money, and they want you to pay for it. E&O typically helps with the cost of defending the claim and, if the claim succeeds, the amount you owe, up to the limit you chose.

Who usually needs it

Any business that sells knowledge, advice, or a professional service should think about it. That includes consultants, accountants and bookkeepers, IT support and software providers, marketing and design firms, engineers, and home inspectors. Many clients now ask for proof of E&O before they sign a contract, and some industries require it. If your work shapes decisions, designs, systems, or finances, a client can blame the outcome on you, and that is exactly what this policy is for.

How it differs from general liability

General liability, usually called CGL, covers a different kind of claim. It responds when someone is hurt or their property is damaged, like a visitor who trips in your office or a contractor who damages a client's wall. E&O covers financial loss from your professional work, where nothing was physically broken. Many businesses need both, because a single project can produce either kind of claim. One policy does not replace the other.

What it usually does not cover

E&O does not cover everything. It typically does not cover deliberate wrongdoing, criminal acts, or fraud. It usually does not cover property damage or injuries, which belong under general liability. It also usually does not cover a client who is simply unhappy with your work and wants a refund, with no financial loss to point to. Most E&O policies only respond to claims made while the policy is in force, so letting coverage lapse can leave past work exposed.

What to check before you buy

Start with the limit, the most the policy will pay on a claim. One million dollars is a common starting point, and some client contracts require more. Check the deductible, the amount you pay first, and make sure you could actually pay it. Ask whether the policy covers the full range of services you offer, including any new service you added this year. And check the retroactive date, the earliest date of work the policy covers. If it is set after work you already did, that earlier work may not be covered.

E&O is not about expecting to make a mistake. It is about knowing that one client disagreement does not have to become a bill you cannot pay. If you sell advice or professional work and you are not sure whether you are covered, message me for a no-pressure second look at what you have now.
