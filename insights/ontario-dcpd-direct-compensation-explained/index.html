DCPD in Ontario: Why Your Own Insurer Pays When Another Driver Hits You

You are sitting at a red light and someone rear-ends you. Your own insurer pays to fix your car, and that is exactly how Ontario auto insurance is designed to work.

You are sitting at a red light and someone rear-ends you. Your car needs repairs, and the other driver is clearly at fault. So why does your own insurance company end up paying the bill? The answer is a part of every standard Ontario auto policy called Direct Compensation, or DCPD. Once you understand it, the claims process after a crash makes a lot more sense, and you will know exactly what to expect.

What DCPD actually is

DCPD stands for Direct Compensation for Property Damage. It is a mandatory part of every standard auto policy in Ontario, which means it is already on your policy whether you asked for it or not. Here is the plain version. When another driver is at fault for a crash and damages your car, your own insurer pays to fix it, instead of you having to chase the other driver's insurer. The two insurers sort out the money between themselves afterwards. You deal with one company, yours, and that is the whole point.

This is different from how things work in some other places, where you claim against the at-fault driver's insurer directly. In Ontario, your insurer handles your claim and then recovers what it paid from the other driver's insurer behind the scenes.

When DCPD applies

DCPD applies when three things are true. First, the crash happened in Ontario. Second, at least one other vehicle was involved, and that vehicle is insured. Third, you were not at fault, or only partly at fault. Fault is decided using Ontario's Fault Determination Rules, a set of standard rules for common crash situations, like a rear-end collision. Your insurer applies those rules to the facts of your crash and assigns a fault percentage to each driver.

If the other driver is fully at fault, DCPD covers the full cost of repairing your vehicle. If you are partly at fault, DCPD covers the share that matches the other driver's fault. For example, if you are found 25 percent at fault, DCPD covers 75 percent of the damage, and the remaining share is where your collision coverage comes in, if you bought it.

What DCPD covers, and what it does not

DCPD covers damage to your vehicle and its contents, such as items inside the car that were damaged in the crash. It also typically covers loss of use, which means the cost of a rental car or other transport while your vehicle is being repaired. If the other driver is fully at fault, you usually pay no deductible under DCPD. If you are partly at fault, a deductible may apply to your share, depending on your policy.

There are limits to what DCPD does. It covers property damage only, not injuries. Injuries are handled under the accident benefits part of your policy. DCPD also does not help if the crash happened outside Ontario, or if the other driver was uninsured. And if you were fully at fault, DCPD does not apply at all. That is the situation collision coverage is for, if you chose to buy it.

What to do after a crash

The steps are simple. Exchange information with the other driver, including names, licence plate numbers, and insurance details. Take photos of the damage and the scene if it is safe to do so. Report the crash to your insurer promptly, even if the damage looks minor. Your insurer will determine fault using the standard rules, arrange the repair, and handle the recovery from the other insurer.

DCPD exists so that a crash caused by someone else does not leave you chasing another insurer for months. Your own company pays, fixes the car, and sorts out the rest.
