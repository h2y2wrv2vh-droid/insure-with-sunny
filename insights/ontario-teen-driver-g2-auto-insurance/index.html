Your Teen Just Got Their G2: What Happens to Your Ontario Auto Insurance

A G2 licence changes your household's auto insurance picture. Here is how Ontario parents should handle it, from listing your teen properly to the discounts that keep costs under control.

That moment is exciting and a little nerve-wracking: your teenager walks in with a G2 licence and the keys suddenly matter more than ever. What a lot of Ontario parents do not realize is that the moment a licensed teen lives in your household and has access to your car, your auto insurance picture changes. Here is how it works in Ontario, and the steps that keep you protected and your costs under control.

Occasional drivers must be listed on the policy

In Ontario, anyone who lives in your household and holds a licence with regular access to a vehicle is generally expected to be listed on that vehicle's insurance policy. That includes a teen with a G2, even a teen who "only drives once in a while." The distinction insurers draw is between occasional and principal drivers:

  • A principal driver is the person who drives the vehicle most often. If your teen gets their own car, they are usually the principal driver on it.
  • An occasional driver is someone who drives the car sometimes but is not the main driver. A teen driving the family car on weekends is the classic example.

Either way, the teen should be disclosed. This is not a formality. If your teen causes a collision and the insurer was never told they were a regular driver of the vehicle, a misrepresentation or non-disclosure issue can arise at claim time, and that is the last thing you want when your kid is already shaken up.

Yes, the premium will go up

Let us be honest: adding a newly licensed driver usually increases the premium. Young drivers have far less experience, and insurers price for that risk. But the increase is often smaller than parents fear, and there are real levers to pull:

  • Good student or education discounts. Many Ontario insurers offer a discount for full-time students under 25 with strong grades, usually a B average or better. Ask your broker whether your insurer offers one and what proof they need, typically a recent report card.
  • Driver training certificate. A certificate from an MTO-approved beginner driver education course can reduce premiums with many insurers. The course also genuinely makes new drivers safer, so it is worth doing for reasons beyond the discount.
  • Keep the teen as an occasional driver on one car. Assigning the teen as a principal driver on a second or third vehicle costs more than listing them as an occasional driver on one family car, assuming they do not need daily use of their own car.
  • Telematics or usage-based programs. Several Ontario insurers offer apps that monitor driving habits and discount careful driving. For a cautious teen, this can meaningfully bring the cost down over time.
  • Raise deductibles cautiously. A higher deductible can lower premiums, but make sure the deductible is an amount you can actually pay on short notice if your teen is in a fender bender.

Revisit your liability limits

When a teen starts driving, your liability limit matters more than ever. A serious at-fault collision can trigger claims that run far beyond the province's minimum requirements. Talk to your broker about whether your current limits still feel comfortable now that a less experienced driver is behind the wheel regularly. This is also a good time to review your accident benefits options, since the province's standard benefits changed in July 2026 and additional coverage is available.

One car or two?

If your household has two vehicles and a new teen driver, the question of which car they drive most often matters. Insurers rate each vehicle based on who principally drives it. If the teen mostly takes the older sedan to school and back, that car should be rated with them as an occasional driver, while the higher-value vehicle stays with the most experienced principal driver. Getting the assignment right keeps the pricing honest and avoids claim complications.

Should a teen's older car carry collision coverage?

Many families hand the new driver the oldest vehicle in the driveway. That raises a practical question: is collision coverage worth it on a car that might be worth only a few thousand dollars? There is no one-size answer. Collision coverage pays to repair or replace your own vehicle after an at-fault crash, which is exactly the scenario a new driver is most likely to face. But if the car is fully paid off and inexpensive, the premium for collision can approach the car's value over time. This is a judgement call worth making with your broker, not on your own, since dropping it removes protection you cannot add back after an accident.

Put a date on the calendar for a renewal review

A teen's rating usually improves as they gain claim-free years and move through the graduated licensing system from G2 to a full G licence. That means the conversation should not end today. Mark each renewal to revisit the policy: ask whether the teen still qualifies as an occasional driver, whether telematics data has earned further discount, and whether the vehicle assignments still reflect who drives what. Insurance is a moving picture once a teen is in the household, and the cheapest compliant setup changes as they mature as drivers.

Have the conversation before the first solo drive

The best time to call your broker is the week your teen gets their G2, not the week after the first claim. A quick review lets you update the policy, confirm the teen is listed correctly, and capture any discounts they qualify for. Bring the licence, the report card, and any driver training certificate to that conversation so nothing gets missed.
